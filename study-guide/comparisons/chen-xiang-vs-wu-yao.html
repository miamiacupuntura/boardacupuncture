Herb comparison

Aquilariae Lignum resinatum (chen xiang) vs. Linderae Radix (wu yao)

Study tip · Clues to help answer

ClueChen XiangWu Yao
Shared cluesBoth warm the Spleen and Kidneys, mobilize Qi, disperse Cold, and treat chest/abdominal fullness with wheezing from rebellious Qi.
Fast distinctionBitter downward action, stronger Kidney warming, helps the Kidneys accept Qi.Highly mobile action throughout the Triple Burner for Cold, Qi blockage or congealed Blood.

Comparative answer key

CompareChen Xiang (Aquilariae Lignum resinatum)Wu Yao (Linderae Radix)
CategoryHerbs that Regulate the Qi.
Properties/channelsWarm, acrid, bitter and aromatic; Stomach, Kidney and Spleen.Warm and acrid; Urinary Bladder, Lung, Kidney and Spleen.
Movement of QiBitter quality drains and directs rebellious Qi downward.Mobile quality reaches broadly and addresses pathogenic rebellious disorders throughout the Triple Burner.
Kidney clueWarms the Kidneys more strongly and helps them accept Qi.Treats frequent urination or incontinence associated with Kidney deficiency.
Clinical emphasisAbdominal distention/pain, nausea, vomiting, wheezing from excess or deficiency, and constipation or dysuria from Qi stagnation.Chest/abdominal fullness and pain, vomiting, lower-abdominal pain, Cold Shan, Qi blockage and Cold-congealed Blood.
Formula cluesZi Xue Dan; Su He Xiang Wan; Gun Tan Wan.Bei Xie Fen Qing Yin; Tian Tai Wu Yao San; Ge Xia Zhu Yu Tang; Suo Quan Wan.
Shared formulaSi Mo Tang, a formula for directing rebellious Qi downward.
Name clueAloeswood or Aquilaria wood.Three-Nerved Spicebush Root or Lindera root.

Study guide · Exam focus

Why it mattersThe herbs share warmth, Kidney/Spleen channels and rebellious-Qi indications; their direction and breadth separate them.
Common confusionChen Xiang emphasizes downward Qi and Kidney acceptance; Wu Yao emphasizes broad mobility through the Triple Burner.
Historical/source notesThe exam source associates Chen Xiang incense with psychiatric disease and describes Wu Yao as a “king herb” for women's problems and Wind- or Qi-stroke. These statements are retained as source material, not clinical recommendations.
Caution and dose in sourceThe source lists Qi deficiency and internal Heat as contraindications and 3–9 g as a shared range. This page is not a prescribing guide.
SourceChinese Herbal Medicine: Materia Medica, Third Edition, Dan Bensky, Steven Clavey, Erich Stöger and Andrew Gamble (2004), p. 537. Source Exam Key CMID 8915.
